# Truss — logo brief

**This is a commissioning document, not an asset.** Nothing here is drawn yet.
Hand this to a designer or a high-end image model and get back something that
looks like a photographed or draughted *object*, not a vector zigzag.

The wordmark is settled and approved. **Only the mark needs making.**

---

## What is already decided

### The wordmark — approved, do not redesign

**TRUSS**, uppercase, geometric sans, heavy weight, wide letterspacing
(≈ 0.2 em), held between two horizontal rules:

- **Rule above** — thick (≈ 3 px at 40 px cap height). This is the top chord.
- **Rule below** — thin (≈ 1.5 px), roughly half the weight of the one above.
  This is the bottom chord.
- Both rules run to the exact optical width of the word. Never wider, never
  narrower. *The structure is sized to its load.*
- The word sits between them. **The word is the web.**

That asymmetry — heavy above, light below — is not a style choice. It is the
whole product: a hard ceiling, a softer floor, and everything that matters
living in the space between them.

The wordmark works. Leave it alone.

### The one rule that governs everything

> **Nothing crosses the top chord.**

No element, no glow, no letterform, no crop, no lens flare, no stray rivet. The
web rises to meet that line and stops. This is the rule of the mark and the rule
of the system, and they are deliberately the same rule. Any comp that violates
it is rejected regardless of how good it looks.

---

## What the mark must be

A **five-panel Warren truss in elevation**, rendered as a real engineered object
with real material, real joints and real light.

The previous attempt failed because it was a zigzag between two lines — a
diagram of a truss, not a truss. The difference between those two things is
entirely in the details listed below, and those details are the brief.

### Geometry — fixed, not up for interpretation

- **Five upward apexes.** Five, because there are five members. Not four, not
  six. A viewer who counts should find the system.
- **Ten diagonals**, alternating up-down-up-down across the span, meeting the
  chords at consistent angles (roughly 55–62° from horizontal).
- **Top chord**: one continuous unbroken member across the whole span. The
  single heaviest, brightest element in the composition.
- **Bottom chord**: continuous, visibly lighter section, quieter in value.
- **Camber**: the entire span bows **upward by about 3% of its length** at
  midspan. Both chords carry the same curve. This is real fabrication practice —
  a truss is built with an upward curve so that under full design load it settles
  level. Most viewers will never consciously see it. They will see that the mark
  does not look like clip art.
- **Aspect ratio** roughly 2.4 : 1 (wide). It is a span, not a badge.

### The details that make it real

This is the part that separates the deliverable from the thing I already threw
away. Every item is load-bearing:

1. **Gusset plates at every joint.** The polygonal steel plates where members
   meet — pentagon or shield shaped, slightly larger than the members they join,
   with visible edges and a hint of thickness. *This is the single most
   important detail.* A truss without gussets is a zigzag; a truss with them is a
   structure.
2. **Rivet or bolt patterns on the gussets.** Six to twelve per plate, in
   regular arcs or grids, correctly scaled — small, crisp, catching a highlight
   on the upper edge and dropping a micro-shadow below. If a viewer can count the
   rivets, we have won.
3. **Members with section depth.** Not lines. The chords should read as real
   structural sections — I-beam or box — with a visible flange face and a hint of
   the web behind it. Diagonals as angle sections with visible thickness. Each
   member gets a micro-bevel on its lit edge.
4. **Honest fabrication.** Slight variation in weld or plate edges. Mill scale.
   A very subtle surface grain in the steel. Nothing distressed or grungy — this
   is precision-made, maintained, and about eight years old.
5. **The loaded joint.** The midspan top joint — where a truss works hardest —
   is **incandescent**. Warm amber, as if the load itself has heat in it. Light
   spills onto the two adjacent gussets, throws the faintest warm bounce along
   the underside of the top chord, and falls off to nothing within two panels.
   It is the only warm thing in the image and the only light source inside the
   composition.

### Material and light

- **Steel**: cold-rolled, desaturated blue-grey, semi-matte. Not chrome. Not
  polished. Not brushed-aluminium-product-render.
- **Key light**: the loaded joint itself, warm (≈ 2700 K), low intensity, tight
  falloff.
- **Fill**: cool ambient from above and slightly left, enough to model the
  sections without flattening them.
- **Rim**: a hard bright line along the *upper* edge of the top chord. This is
  what makes the cap read as the hardest, brightest, most absolute element.
- **Ground**: deep near-black (`#0B0D12`), or the same with a blueprint grid at
  3–4% opacity. No vignette. No gradient sky. No floor plane. No reflection.

Two rules carried straight from the product into the artwork:

- **Amber never means error.** It means *working, under load*. Nothing red
  appears in the mark, ever.
- **The top chord is the brightest thing in frame.** Nothing out-values it, not
  even the glow.

## Three directions to comp

Produce all three, then choose. They are genuinely different bets.

### A — Engineering plate *(primary recommendation)*

Fine-line technical illustration in the register of a structural engineering
handbook or a patent drawing. Precise ink linework, hatching to indicate section
cuts, consistent line weights that follow drafting convention (heavy for cut
edges, medium for visible edges, hairline for hidden). The loaded joint is the
one place colour enters an otherwise monochrome plate.

Optionally: faint drafting furniture — a dimension line under the span, small
numbered callouts at two or three joints, a node label. Kept at very low
contrast so it reads as texture rather than clutter.

**Why this wins:** it is unmistakably *drawn by someone who knows what a truss
is*. It survives at any size, prints on anything, reproduces in one colour, and
is the least likely of the three to look generated.

### B — Rendered steel

Photographic-grade 3D render, orthographic or near-orthographic elevation, with
everything from the "details that make it real" list physically present.
Shallow depth of field is **not** wanted — this is a technical object, held in
full focus.

**Risk:** this is the direction that becomes generic product-render slop if the
gussets and rivets are not right. Judge every comp on the joints, not the
silhouette.

### C — Isometric, showing depth

Three-quarter view revealing that the truss is actually *two* parallel trusses
joined by cross-bracing — which is what real bridge trusses are. Conveys
"separate things bound into one structure" more literally than any flat view can,
and gives the etymology (*trousse*, a bundle) something to point at.

**Risk:** harder to read at small sizes, and the extra geometry can muddy at
favicon scale. Would need a simplified small-size variant regardless.

## Hard nos

- **No zigzag between two lines.** That was the last attempt and it is why this
  document exists.
- No rotation, ever. A truss has an up. A tilted truss is a collapsed truss.
- No non-uniform scaling — it changes the member angles, which in a real truss
  changes the forces.
- No lens flare, bloom, sparkles, particles, or light rays.
- No circuit-board traces, no glowing hex grids, no "AI" motifs, no data
  streams, no globes, no lightning bolts, no power-button glyphs, no leaves.
- No gradient mesh backgrounds, no vignette, no floor reflection.
- Nothing red.
- Nothing crossing the top chord.

## How to judge a comp in ten seconds

1. Count the apexes. Five?
2. Look at one joint. Is there a gusset plate with rivets on it, or is it just
   two lines touching?
3. Does anything cross the top chord?
4. Is the top chord the brightest thing in frame?
5. Is the amber *inside* the composition — a joint under load — or is it
   generic rim lighting sprayed over everything?

If any answer is wrong, it is not close. Send it back with the specific item.
